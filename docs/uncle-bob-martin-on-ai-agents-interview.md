# Uncle Bob Martin on Coding with AI Agents

*A live-stream interview with Robert C. "Uncle Bob" Martin. Lightly formatted from the
transcript into speaker turns for readability — wording is preserved, only obvious
verbal-transcription artifacts were tidied. Speakers: **Host** and **Uncle Bob**.*

---

**Host:** I've got a treat for you today. I've got someone who I've been wanting to speak to for a while — someone who's been really into software for as long as I've been a developer, much longer than I've been a developer, and someone who's now making his mark on the agent space. We have Uncle Bob Martin, live and in his bathrobe, ready to throw down. Uncle Bob, hello. Welcome.

**Uncle Bob:** Hello, and thank you. Good to be here.

**Host:** It's great to have you. For folks who don't know the bathrobe thing specifically, we should probably caveat that. What's going on there, and why has the bathrobe become an important part of your character?

**Uncle Bob:** It happened, I think, two years ago. I was on my front porch in my bathrobe. It was 6:00 in the morning and I started thinking about how awful SQL was — because of all the SQL injections. It makes no sense to have a textual language as your database access, for security reasons. I was in my bathrobe at the time and I just pulled out my phone and I ranted, and that became the morning bathrobe rant. Apparently it was popular, so I did it a few more times.

**Host:** So is this the mood you've come here in today? Is this the Uncle Bob —

**Uncle Bob:** It's early in the morning, I haven't had my coffee, and just don't bother me. [Later:] It's actually 10 in the morning, the bathrobe's off, and I'm on my first Diet Coke, so I'm fine.

**Host:** Very good. So what's Uncle Bob's story? Most of my audience are developers, but some are not. How do we introduce the phenomenon that is Uncle Bob, especially to non-developers?

**Uncle Bob:** Phenomenon — I don't know about that. I'm a programmer. I've been a programmer for a very long time, over half a century at this point. My first program was in 1964, when I was 12. The program was on a little model computer that my mother had bought me for my 12th birthday. You programmed it by putting little white tubes on pegs. It was essentially a three-bit finite state machine, but it fascinated the hell out of me at the age of 12.

**Host:** So you're 12 years old. How do we get to 50 years later, Uncle Bob doing his thing now?

**Uncle Bob:** A little more than 50. I just started learning as much as I could about programming. My father bought me a book about Fortran, a book about COBOL, a book about PL/1. I read them all. I had no machines to execute anything on, so I wrote programs on paper and executed them in my head. I got a job writing a little bit of code at the age of 16 — but that was temporary. Then I got a real job at 18, and I've been a programmer ever since.

**Host:** So you've been in the trenches a long time, and you wrote a rather important book, I think.

**Uncle Bob:** A few. One of them I think was important. I've written a few more, but one of them seemed to take off. That was a nice one.

**Host:** The cleanliness of code, if I remember correctly?

**Uncle Bob:** Yeah — *Clean Code.* Here's the second edition.

**Host:** It's maybe the most — certainly when I have conversations about good software-engineering books, it's the one that most often gets quoted back at me. Incredibly popular, incredibly influential. That's why I'm interested to talk to you today: you've had this big influence in the pre-AI era, and you've been working in the pre-AI era for almost as long as it's existed. How have things changed for you now that AI is out there and is a thing?

**Uncle Bob:** This took me a little by surprise around December of last year, Christmas time. I was just fiddling around. I'd already been playing with ChatGPT, a little bit of Grok, a little bit of this and that, and I hadn't been real impressed. Then I went through a period where I thought, well, maybe these things are a little more interesting than I thought. I got an agent — the first one was an early Grok. I just asked it to write me some code, and it did a kind of poor job, but it wrote the code. I was in the middle of a project at the time, so I said, well, maybe this thing can help me. I started having it work with me on the project, and I was cleaning up after it all the time. It was always making a mess, always leaving little dog-do behind. And I thought: it's interesting because it's fast, but it's frustrating because it makes me slow.

Then I started thinking — wait a minute, because it's fast, it can do things that I cannot. In the very early 2000s there were a couple of innovations that caught me. I thought, ooh, these are good ideas, but they were completely impractical.

One was called **CRAP** — it's an acronym, what can I say. It was a way to take test coverage: you'd run coverage over your code, and you'd also measure the cyclomatic complexity of every function, and you'd mix those two in a complicated formula, and out would come a score — a measure of how crappy your function was. Way back in the early 2000s I thought this was a great idea, and I ran it over a big project. There was a bunch of crappy functions, but it took me forever to go through every one and fix them and rewrite the tests. It was all working, so I didn't think I could afford to do that. Interesting, but I set it aside.

Another innovation was **mutation testing**. This one really caught my attention. You have a little program that runs through your source code and flips negative signs to positive, less-than to greater-than, equals to not-equals, and other things. For each flip, it runs your entire test suite and expects it to fail — obviously something significant changed. If it doesn't fail, that's a surviving mutant, and it must be killed. I ran this over that same project around the year 2000. I had to run it overnight — the test suite ran for four minutes and I was running it several hundred times. It came up with a bunch of surviving mutants I was able to fix, but again, it was impractical. I could not put that in a normal build.

So I'm sitting there last December — maybe January by then — thinking: wait a minute. These agents are fast, they don't care how boring the work is, and they'll do what I tell them. So why don't you run CRAP over everything you've just done? And it would run CRAP and clean up the code. And why don't you run mutation testing too? And it would — maybe 30 minutes instead of an overnight run — and then plug all the holes and make sure there were tests covering everything. I thought: this might be a way to clean up the dog-do. These things leave a lot behind, but maybe this is a good way to clean it up.

So I continued on this path — adding more tools, working with the agents — and I got them to a place where they were doing a pretty good job. Nowadays my principle is: I set the agents working, I have them run these tools, and I'm going to work very hard to get to a situation where I don't have to look at the code at all. I can trust what they do. I do other things to verify the code is still decent — I'll look at the CRAP scores and make sure they're low, I'll do spot checks, I have a whole bunch of other tests I run. But overall that's the goal. If these things are fast — and they are — and if I can constrain them to do a good job, then I am not going to impose my slowness upon them. My bonus is reviewing or inspecting the code at some level of detail. They are fast with code; I am slow with code. So I'm going to let them have the code, and I'll deal with the stuff around it to make sure it's all okay.

**Host:** So your goal is to pull yourself away from the code, away from human review, and construct scaffolding around the code so you don't have to interact with it much yourself — and so the agent is constrained as much as possible, in as tight a straitjacket as possible, so it can't make a mistake. There's an assumption there I want to touch on first: the word "dog-do." Why is dog-do bad? Why is bad code bad? Why do we care, if these things are so fast and can move so quickly? Why bother with all this harness? Can't we just push through until the bugs disappear?

**Uncle Bob:** One thing I noticed really early — December-ish — was that I'd have the little Grok agent do something, and it would leave a mess. Instead of cleaning it, I'd have it do the next thing, and it would leave even more mess, then the next thing. I noticed it slowing down and having difficulty. It would get into a mode where it would change one thing but inadvertently break another, then fix that but break another. It started going around in circles. And I thought: okay, these agents may be fast and relatively smart, but they are as subject as humans are to messy code. Maybe not *as* subject — maybe there's a difference in threshold — but the threshold is still there. The code can get messy enough that the agents cannot deal with it any longer, and then they just start to spin and make the mess even worse. I've actually had them just give up. One agent one time said, in effect, "I just can't deal with this anymore." Paraphrasing — it didn't use those words, but that was obviously what was going on.

**Host:** So your response is: how can I clean up the crap? When most people encounter that, they start loading the agent with instructions — piling information into `claude.md` or `agents.md`, and every time they see something bad they use an instruction. Whereas you're using a deterministic mechanism, automated checks. In my terminology that's *steering* — trying to steer it. Are you doing any steering, or how does that work in your mindset?

**Uncle Bob:** Initially I started with that. My early prompts were: here's how you do test-driven development, here's how you do clean code, here's what your code should look like, follow all these rules. Eventually you come up with a document five or ten pages long describing all the good things about code. I suppose I could have fed the whole *Clean Code* book into it. But what I noticed was that the agents — the models — treat those rules in the *Pirates of the Caribbean* sense: they're more like guidelines.

**Host:** Can I credit you — that's exactly the metaphor that came into my head as well. Glad we're aligned there.

**Uncle Bob:** They definitely soften, and there are technical reasons. I did some research into why the models behave this way, and it turns out there's a phenomenon known as **lost in the middle**. As the context window builds up, the stuff at the very beginning and the very end have more prominence than the stuff in the middle, for technical reasons. The agent ignores the stuff in the middle. Anything you say at the very beginning gets shoved into the middle if the prompt is long. Maybe the first three sentences remain priority, but the 50th and 80th sentences are gone — just in the middle somewhere. The poor agent is trying to deal with this massive, ever-growing context, trying to pull the important bits out, and the stuff in the middle is just gone.

Deterministic tools don't disappear that way. The key with agents — and this is really hard to do — is to trim that initial prompt down to its absolute minimum, so you get as much of it as possible into its priority. And then do deterministic tools after the fact.

**Host:** Totally. I refer to this as the *smart zone* and the *dumb zone* of the context window — not my term, that's Dex Hardy's term, I stole it. Especially the early part, the first 150k tokens, it's pretty smart. But as you go along, the attention relationships in the transformer get really strained and diluted. It's like every token is shouting in a crowded room, and the room's getting more crowded — you can't hear the signal for the noise. So you've rejected steering and gone back to some old techniques — automated checks. Because those checks don't go into the context window the way steering instructions do, you can just layer them on, up and up. If you're using a language with strong types, and tests — how does that picture look? Is there ever too much when it comes to automated checks?

**Uncle Bob:** That's one of the things I'm working very hard to figure out. Obviously there has to be a case where there's too much — eventually you slow the agents down to the point where they're slower than humans, and then you've lost the game. But as long as you can keep the margin of productivity higher than a human, you're still ahead. From what I've seen so far, I can get that margin — like a factor of two, three, or four. They'll still go pretty fast, even though I'm slowing them down a lot.

When you use these deterministic tools, what you're really doing is putting them into a loop. Lots of people like to talk about loops nowadays. You're saying: you must change the code until this tool says it's okay. Now the agent is going around and around — I've got to do this, add more tests, cut the cyclomatic complexity, split these functions apart — until it gets into conformance. So you are sacrificing productivity for higher quality, and at some point that's got to give way. But I haven't found the endpoint yet.

Now I'm in the midst of trying to get multiple agents talking to each other and handing off — one guy does one thing, the next reviews it, the next tests it, the next hardens it. There's communication overhead like crazy, and yet it's still faster by a large token than a human.

**Host:** Let's talk about multi-agent systems, because I find that super fascinating. I've always had a bit of a suspicion of people who say, "I've got rid of all my workforce, I now have a hundred agents, every one has a different role, they all talk to each other, they've got their own email accounts" — all that rubbish. What's always worked for me — and I make an exception for implementation and then review — is something like a red-green-refactor approach: the implementer just writes the failing test and makes it work, it doesn't have to be beautiful. Then the reviewer comes in — it doesn't have to do the exploration because it's already got the diff, it knows exactly what it's reviewing, and you can pile in a lot more steering instructions because the task is less constrained. So I'm interested in your take — what the implementer does, the reviewer, and these hardener agents. Give me more on that.

**Uncle Bob:** There are two advantages to multiple agents. One: you can run them in parallel — three coders at the same time, and my little laptop can support a lot more than three. Two: when you focus the agents down to a single task, you keep the context window under control, and the lost-in-the-middle problem becomes much less of a problem. You can pile a few more rules — not a lot more — at the top, and they'll follow them better. You can also set up a system where agents are born, do the task, and die, so the next one comes in with a clean context.

The disadvantages: startup times are high. An agent takes 10, 15 seconds to start up, and then it's got to figure out its whole context all over again.

I like to focus the task as much as I can. So I run a **specifier**. Its job is to take a human-written document and turn it into a Gherkin file and a QA procedure. Gherkin is your given-when-then stuff — a high-level acceptance test. A QA procedure is essentially a system test — you run the system through the UI. I have it write from a human's point of view: you are a human, you are operating this system at the UI, you must prove the system works. That produces two documents.

Those feed into a **coder**. The coder writes unit tests and the code that implements the described story, and also gets the Gherkin working. Once that's working, it feeds into a **cleaner**, whose job is to run CRAP analysis and general code review and clean up whatever mess the implementer made — because the implementer will have made a horrible mess by that point. From there it goes to a **hardener**, who runs the mutation testing and is absolutely merciless: it's going to mutate it, get 100% coverage, every equal sign and every less-than. That takes a good long time. Then it goes into a **QA agent**, which takes the written QA document, turns it into an executable script that manipulates the system, and comes up with a deterministic result. If you get through all that, you've got a pretty well-working program.

I've had a lot of success with this. If I give it a task that takes a single agent five minutes to complete with questionable results, this takes about an hour to go through all that — which is still a benefit, because a person would take about half a day. So maybe a factor of four or five improvement in productivity, and very high quality, much more than the human would put into it. And you're spending productivity early to gain it later — this is an investment in your own codebase.

**Host:** What I found really interesting is that it's not only the context window you're manipulating — there's this idea of a *trajectory* of a context window. If you get the agent to do one thing and steer it a certain way, everything that follows in that same session continues following that trajectory. If you get it to say, "okay, maybe we should test the UI here," then every single time it will test the UI again, no matter how many changes you make. The only way to clear the trajectory is to clear the context window. So an implementer agent, just trying to get it working, has a less harsh trajectory than one trying to make sure you have 100% coverage. Does that click with your mental model?

**Uncle Bob:** Yeah, it certainly does. There's a well-known effect with these models, and it happens with non-programmers too. You're talking to a model about the best coffee to have and how to brew it nicely — a nice little conversation — and then somebody walks by talking about the latest soap opera, and that gets into the context window. You didn't put it there. But from that point on, all the coffee references have to do with the soap opera. The model can't differentiate. So your idea of trajectory is pretty good: as long as you keep the direction of the model unconfused — it knows the direction it's going in, everything in the context window is consistent — then it's not going to have these crazy hallucinations people often deal with.

**Host:** Or even just misalignment. Hallucination is always a danger, an accepted danger, and your approach — this gauntlet of different agents slamming horrendous stuff at it — is a great way of taking that out. You've talked a lot about the implementation phase. What I'm also interested in is: what planning do you do up front, and how are you thinking about the internal structure of your codebases? Having a good test suite is good, mutation testing is good — but if you've got badly designed APIs, badly shaped modules, how does that interact with all these automated checks? How much are you thinking about that?

**Uncle Bob:** Up to the last month or so, I was doing that part manually — but still using the agents. I'd have the agents build me up a nice thing, then interrogate them: what's the structure here? How does this module interrelate with that one? What are the modules, after all, and how do they talk to each other? I'd ask those questions and then get scared to death, because the answers were horribly frightening. Then I'd design a module structure and tell the agent, here's how the modules should really be partitioned, here's how you should communicate with them, and give them an implementation plan to implement.

That's a tough one. So I had my agents build me an **architecture viewer** — I can pop up a nice little UML diagram showing the modular structure and where the dependencies run. I can click on a module and see inside it to the submodules, click on those, and it'll pop the code up on screen. I can drill down as much as I want and view the system architecture at any level. That was really useful.

I've also put together a deterministic tool where I can define which module should depend on which, and how the dependency should flow. That goes into a nice tight little specification file the agents cannot violate. Another checker runs at the end, and if they violate it, they've got to fix it — usually by inverting a dependency, inserting an interface, or splitting a module in half. It keeps my rules from being violated. I'm working now to see if I can automate that, and I'm having not a lot of luck so far.

**Host:** I'm basically in exactly the same boat — you get so much leverage from well-designed modules. Could you explain what that leverage is? Why is having a good structure for these modules important?

**Uncle Bob:** It's the same argument we had for dirty code versus clean code. Anything that is well partitioned, with well-disciplined interfaces between the parts, is something a human can grasp, because we compartmentalize in our minds well. So do the models, so do the agents — maybe at a slightly different threshold. They work far better if they can focus on a module, and if that module has a trajectory, so the model doesn't get confused by the topics inside it. (I'm using "model" and "module" — I want to be clear.) It's the coffee-and-soap-opera argument. If you load up a module with every bit of stuff under the sun, the poor agent is going to wonder, "What am I doing in here? How do I do anything in here?" If you compartmentalize nicely, it works pretty well — just like a human.

**Host:** And you're probably getting better value out of your test suite too. Here's a related question. I'm a huge fan of your work, but I'm also a huge fan of John Ousterhout's work — his concept of **deep modules** is something I find fascinating. You can have shallow modules, with a wide interface and not much hidden inside, and deep modules, with a small interface and lots hidden inside. It occurs to me that's really good for models, because they can read the interface without having to understand the implementation. Does that chime with you?

**Uncle Bob:** Yeah, absolutely. The models pay attention to interface names, they pay attention to structure. It can allow them to not read the code beneath them — which is both a danger and an advantage. As long as the code is consistent, you're okay. They also pay attention to the tests — they read tests to understand what the system does. So anything you can do that helps the structure of the code will help the models understand it. By the way, in this book there's a long debate between me and John Ousterhout in the appendix, and it was a lot of fun. He and I had a blast — well, I don't know how much fun he had, but I had a lot of fun.

**Host:** I watched the entire thing. I saw you guys interviewed on a really great YouTube discussion where you talked about it, and I loved it. That's kind of the reason I wanted you to come on, actually. Okay — is there anything in your book you'd change or update now? I'm thinking about advice like "create small functions" and "keep functions small." Is there anything we just need to throw out that's done? I don't like asking this because it puts a lot of pressure on you — but do you have an answer?

**Uncle Bob:** So, **thresholds**, for one thing. It seems to me the agents can deal with different levels of complexity than humans. They have a much better short-term memory — a huge, perfectly accurate short-term memory. So one thing I do is widen the allowed size of a function, by adjusting the CRAP score. For a human I'd keep CRAP numbers below four; for the agents I've set this at six, and I'm thinking maybe I'll push it to eight. I'm trying to find where the threshold is, and it's not an easy threshold to find.

**Host:** What does four-to-eight look like — a 20-line function, a 100-line function?

**Uncle Bob:** It really boils down to the cyclomatic complexity — the number of pathways through the function. If you've got 100% coverage, a CRAP score of six means there are six pathways through the function, all covered with tests. That's really the goal of CRAP: get it all covered with tests, then limit the cyclomatic complexity. I've had a number of debates with the agents — and by the way, you can't trust any debate you have with an agent, but I still have them anyway — and they seem to think six is probably pretty good. Well, you don't really trust them, but okay. I think there's a threshold difference there.

There's another factor. In my books I talk about **disciplines**. One was test-driven development. I'm a big advocate of TDD — but that's a human discipline, done because humans are wired a certain way. I cannot and will not enforce that on the agents. I don't think it makes any sense to make an agent write a single line of a test, then a single line of production code, then the next line of the test. For a human it does. So I allow the agents to behave more like John Ousterhout would — write a function, then write the test for that function, then the next function, then its test. They always fall back on doing that, even when I've told them to do TDD at high discipline. So I figure that's probably okay. The bottom line: it's probably a mistake to impose a human *discipline* on an agent. It is not a mistake to impose human *values* on the agent — but there may be thresholds we need to change. The disciplines themselves, the behaviors, I don't think it's wise to impose those.

**Host:** That's a lovely way of phrasing it. It's a short-term-memory thing — TDD is great when you have very low short-term memory, like humans. You have just enough to write the test, and just enough to make it pass; you can go get a coffee. Okay — we've talked a lot about building the thing. What do you do *before* you kick things off to your specifier? How much planning are you doing before you go into this gauntlet loop? Because putting the wrong work into a gauntlet is super wasteful.

**Uncle Bob:** The temptation is to specify, specify, specify, and then give it to the agent. This is a very old temptation — one we underwent in the '70s. It led us to the waterfall process, and the agile revolution was the answer back to that. I don't know how well we're doing with the agile revolution either, but it was a way to say: wait a minute, all this heavy upfront planning makes a mess, because what comes out at the end doesn't look anything like the plan and never has.

The temptation with agents is the same — plan, plan, then give it to the agent. I've tried this, I've been in the middle of trying it just this week, and it's always a disaster, always the same outcome: you make all these plans, and as the agents run, you the human realize they can't follow the plan because you didn't think of everything and they're not as wise as you. So they're running half-cocked off on some nonsense you have to stop, back up, rewrite the plan, and start over.

So I've given up on that. Let's try the agile approach now. I don't know how this will work out — it might not work all that well — but let them do a story or two, then look at the architecture at the end, and maybe I'll have to manually get involved, sort a few things out, then a few more stories. That might be a better approach. We may never escape that manual organizing step at the end, although I'm trying to figure out a way to do it.

**Host:** I think of it like this: there's a huge chunk of labor that needs to happen. In the old days that was building the thing — days, weeks, months. Now that's shrunk. But the planning up front, and then the review, is still the same. We're expected to do faster iteration cycles, but the stuff that was actually quite hard is still the same and takes the same time. Building the *wrong* thing is something you can still do. A lot of folks are doing this "plan-maxing" thing — they run their spec through seven different agents, get back a better plan, then go implement. How does that sound?

**Uncle Bob:** The agents love to write plans. Oh my goodness, they love it. They'll embellish the plans, and the plans will be gorgeous and beautiful and spell out all kinds of details. And then they fall apart at the end. You can see it in the industry right now — this movement toward spec-driven development. My impression is that's probably not going to work; my experiments didn't work particularly well. I'm thinking now maybe we should look at the agile ideas again — do a little bit, get feedback, do a little bit more, get feedback, reorganize, and so on.

I used to tell this story back when I was doing agile lectures. If it cost you $1 to make a change to a house — including the initial foundation, the roof, everything — how would you build it? Would you hire an architect, pay thousands to come up with the perfect plan, and then pay the contractor a dollar to build it in one shot? Or would you walk up to the contractor and say, "I want the foundation here, that shape" — oh no, that's bad, let's change it — "put the kitchen over here, the living room there" — there's $2 — "oh heck no, let's change those around" — let the kids walk through, the traffic pattern is crappy, move the stairs? Obviously the latter is probably better, and that's what we're looking at right now. It costs a dollar — well, maybe two, maybe five. The cost of change has plummeted to as close to zero as I think we're ever going to get it. So why would you do this upfront planning, when it's expensive? Why wouldn't you just fiddle, fiddle, fiddle until it looks right?

**Host:** I 100% agree, couldn't agree more. The thing I have a massive issue with is the *spec-driven development* label — because what is it? You can have ten different interpretations. Every time you pass information to an agent — is prompt engineering spec-driven development? It kind of is. In the old days you'd say to your colleague, "just fix that loading issue in the header" — is that a specification I've given him? Every time I say "okay, do a little bit of upfront alignment first," that gets called spec-driven development. But for me the difference is: are you *persisting* your specifications? Are you *returning* to them? What's your attitude — do you keep a list of all your specs in the repo?

**Uncle Bob:** No, I do not. The specifications are ephemeral — they go away. They change a lot; I fiddle with them and they go away. There is no equivalent to source code. We humans wrote the source code — that was the final specification. Well, that doesn't exist anymore. There's still source code, but we humans aren't the ones writing it. A lot of people feel that lack — there has to be a human thing that defines everything up front. But in the end, even what the agents produce was produced by humans.

What I've been doing lately is this: instead of creating a specification that defines what I want, I look at the end result and say, well, *that* is the specification. I've got a bunch of tools out there — the CRAP tool runs for Clojure, for Java, for Go; I wrote a few of them (had my agents write them). I've got the mutation tester, my agent harness. What I tell people is: don't download those. I wrote them for me. What you should do is point your agents at them, have the agents look at them, and then build one for you. That's a far better way of specifying the essence of something, and then customizing it to your particular need.

**Host:** What I always find weird about agents is that if you send them something, they read it — which is very different to humans. You can maybe get a 20% hit rate if you send someone a massive specification, and 20% is generous — 5% maybe. If you pass a spec to an agent, they're probably going to read it.

**Uncle Bob:** The opposite side of that is: the things the agents write, the humans don't read.

**Host:** Yes, exactly. They're expecting us to read everything they write, and we just don't. It's so one-sided. Long term, I'm interested in how human relationships get different because we're so used to communicating with agents — I dictate to my agents like I'm talking to a friend. I'm interested in how life imitates art over time. Okay — I think I'm going to ask you maybe the final question, and it's a fairly beefy one. I'm going to go John Ousterhout again, because John has a great definition for different types of programming — tactical versus strategic.

**Uncle Bob:** [pulling the book down] Wait — I've got to pull this book down. It's here somewhere... okay, tactical versus strategic programming.

**Host:** So tactical is the sergeant on the ground, the person fighting the battle. The strategic stuff is the general, directing the course of the war. We both agree that's a good framing. And agents are really good at tactical, really bad at strategic. Now, for people just starting out — AI has eaten all the tactical stuff — how do they learn to do strategic programming? A lot of people here want you to give them your brain: understand why mutation testing is so good, explain these modules. How do they learn that stuff?

**Uncle Bob:** I get this question a lot. I don't have perfect answers, but here's how I think about it. First, the way a programmer should learn — whether in university or somewhere else — should be to write code. You should be writing code for a year — I don't know how long, but you should be writing code, so you know what the agents are dealing with.

The next thing: when you get hired into a company making heavy use of agents, you — the young person just out of training — should be treated like an agent. The lead engineer, the guy being strategic with a bunch of agents running, should look at you as an agent and give you the same kind of tasks the agents have, and subject you to the same deterministic tools the agents have to use. You should spend several months in that state — being horribly unproductive but learning a hell of a lot. By the time you've gone through that gauntlet, maybe you can be trusted to run an agent of your own.

You cannot lose the code entirely. Ten years ago I used to tell people: if you've never written assembly language, spend a weekend writing assembly language, just so you know what's really going on behind the scenes. If all you're doing is writing Java all day, you live in a fantasy world — there's still magic you don't understand. Spend a weekend doing assembly and you'll finally understand what's really going on. I think that remains true during this educational pathway: you've got to go from binary, through assembly, some basic code like C, higher-level code like Python, and then deal with agent work and deterministic tools, and finally be able to strategically run an agent under supervision.

**Uncle Bob:** Software fundamentals matter for the reason they've always mattered.
